Sam Altman published a document yesterday titled "Our Principles." Five principles. Clean language. Easy to skim and dismiss as corporate positioning.

Don't do that.

Read it as a statement about where power is going.

## the five principles, stripped down

OpenAI organized its framework around democratization, empowerment, universal prosperity, resilience, and adaptability. Together they form a single argument: the default trajectory of AGI concentrates power. Wealth, capability, and decision-making flow toward whoever controls the infrastructure. OpenAI says it will resist that.

Whether you trust that claim is a separate conversation. But the claim itself tells you something — even the company building the most powerful AI in the world is acknowledging that consolidation is the natural gravity here.

That's the sentence worth sitting with.

## scrutinizing altman is the wrong move

There are legitimate reasons to be skeptical of OpenAI as the author of these principles. A company that has reshuffled its governance, shifted from nonprofit to capped-profit, and accumulated more capital and compute than most countries is not a neutral party on questions of who holds power.

But that's a distraction. The more important question: are these the right principles? Largely, yes. Democratization. Empowerment. Broad prosperity. Resilience. Adaptability. These are the ideas local institutions and regional planners should be orienting around — not waiting for a San Francisco lab to operationalize for everyone else.

OpenAI setting these principles doesn't mean OpenAI will realize them in the Coachella Valley. It means someone has to do it locally. That someone is not going to be OpenAI.

## the overlap isn't a coincidence

At [AI Coachella Valley](/), we've been developing a Responsible AI Pledge — a set of commitments asking the valley to engage with this technology with intention rather than just reaction. The overlap with what Altman published yesterday is not surprising. Anyone thinking seriously about AI's trajectory arrives at the same questions: who benefits, who decides, and what happens to communities that aren't at the table.

The difference is context. OpenAI is writing for humanity. We're writing for a region where the workers most exposed to AI-driven displacement have the least visibility into what's coming.

Universal prosperity sounds right in a principles document. In the Coachella Valley it means something specific: scheduling systems at local resorts running on AI-generated yield management. Revenue optimization happening without local ownership of those tools. A regional economy that participates in the impact of AI but not yet in the equity of it.

<div class="callout">
      <strong>The uncomfortable truth:</strong> The valley participates in the disruption. It does not yet participate in the ownership. Those are not the same thing.
    </div>

## what a regional version actually requires

Democratization, applied locally, means building institutions here that have a say in how AI gets deployed — not just adopting whatever vendors ship. Empowerment means investing in founders and operators who want to build those tools in the desert, not import them. Universal prosperity means asking whether the workers whose schedules are being optimized around are benefiting from that optimization or absorbing its cost.

Most local institutions aren't asking these questions yet. That's not a criticism — it's an observation about timing. The window to get ahead of this is narrower than it appears.

OpenAI published its principles for AGI development. That's their jurisdiction. Ours is a 45-mile stretch of desert with a growing conversation about what it means to actually build something here.

The question isn't whether OpenAI will deliver on its principles. The question is whether the Coachella Valley will develop its own — and who is going to do that work if not the people already here.

That work is underway at [aicoachellavalley.org](/). It needs more people in the room.
